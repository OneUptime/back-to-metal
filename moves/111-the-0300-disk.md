## Leaving from
- **AWS:** EBS with transparent device replacement — a failing device was replaced beneath you and you were never told.
- **Google Cloud:** Persistent Disk with the same transparency — again handled entirely by the provider.
- **Azure:** Managed Disks with the same again — which is why nobody arriving from a cloud has ever watched a drive die slowly.

## Why this works
A drive that fails cleanly is the easy case: the storage cluster notices, marks it out, and rebuilds. The hard case is the drive that is dying rather than dead — latency climbing, errors being corrected, throughput halving — while every machine still reports itself healthy and the cluster still reports itself available. Nothing above the device notices, and the symptom is an application that is slow for reasons nobody can find. So this Move instruments the device layer directly, and then rehearses the physical replacement against the inventory from Move 110, which is genuinely new work for anyone arriving from a cloud.

## The runbook
1. Collect per-device metrics properly: latency percentiles, wear indicators, available spare against its own threshold, media and integrity errors, and unsafe shutdown counts.
2. Alert on the trends rather than the thresholds. A wear indicator that moves faster than its historical rate is a better signal than one that crosses a line, because the line is usually crossed long after the drive started misbehaving.
3. Wire the storage cluster's slow-operation warnings into the same alerting path. Those warnings are the earliest reliable signal that one device is dragging a pool, and they are frequently ignored because they are not errors.
4. Model the rebuild. When a device is marked out, the cluster backfills, and that backfill uses the fabric sized in Move 12 for workloads rather than for recovery. Measure what a rebuild does to application latency before you need to know.
5. Set the hot-spare policy deliberately. Immediate automatic replacement shortens the degraded window and starts a rebuild at a time nobody chose; a delay lets a human decide. Both are defensible and one has to be written down.
6. Treat firmware as a data-loss concern. Baselines for the machine firmware, the management controller, the network card, the storage adapter and the drives, with a controlled update path, because specific firmware revisions have lost data in real fleets.
7. Drill the replacement end to end: identify the device, map it to a bay through the inventory from Move 110, raise a ticket using the template from Move 35, and confirm the cluster rebuilds. Time it.

## Operator's notes
- **Swap:** Where the estate is small, replacing a suspect device rather than diagnosing it is often cheaper than the engineering time to be certain. The spares from Move 19 exist for this.
- **Do it faster:** Label the bays physically and in the inventory using the same identifier the operating system reports. Translating between two naming schemes at three in the morning is how the wrong drive gets pulled.
- **Watch out:** Pulling the wrong device from a degraded pool can lose data. The ticket template from Move 35 carries a photograph and a bay number precisely because a technician follows it exactly.
- **Leftovers:** Nothing to remove. This is a capability the provider supplied invisibly and you now supply deliberately.

## Rollback
Nothing here changes the running estate, so the instrumentation is reversible by removing it. The point of no return is the physical act of pulling a device, which is why the drill and the ticket template matter more than the alerting does. If the wrong device is pulled, the way back is the cluster's own recovery and, failing that, the restore path from Move 115 — which is the reason that Move exists before this one.
