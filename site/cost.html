What it costs

$100,000 a month becomes $22,776, the extra ops time already in it. That is $926,688 a year, or 67 per cent of everything you spend on it now, with $14,644 of salary counted on both sides.

The reference build is 16 machines and a spare on the shelf, in one cage. Every figure below is a public list price observed while writing, against a bill this size. Substitute your own and the shape does not change.

The comparison, with the salary in it

Three ways to buy the same computers, with every column carrying its own people. The cloud column used to say the salary was already in the bill. It is not: an invoice bills for machines, and the person who upgrades the managed cluster and carries the pager is paid by you either way. Counting them on one side only is the error this whole page exists to avoid, and it does not stop being an error when it flatters the answer we prefer.

Read down a column and it adds up. That is the only claim being made here: not that the number is right for you, but that the arithmetic is checkable and the assumptions are named.

The people rowBoth columns carry it, because both have it. A cloud invoice bills for machines, not for whoever upgrades the managed cluster and carries the pager — about 160 hours a month, or $14,644.
Why owning is booked as dearerEvery first-hand account says the difference is nought — the same people, before and after. The only estimate that is not nought is an outside one at ten to twenty hours, and this takes twenty. The book is declining the most favourable reading of its own evidence, not reporting that a rack is harder work than an invoice.
Where the twenty hours came fromAn outside estimate puts the extra at ten to twenty hours a month; this takes the top of it. We make OneUptime and we made this move, and we measured our own larger fleet at fourteen — that is us showing our working, not an independent source.

Five years, three ways

A month is the wrong window for this decision. Owning is capital on day one and cheap running afterwards; renting is no capital and dearer running; the cloud is no capital and dearest running. Compared a month at a time the capital either vanishes into an amortisation line or sits there looking like the whole story, and neither is what somebody signing the cheque is choosing between. So here is the life of one generation of machines, with the salary counted on all three sides and the capital on the line where it actually happens.

Level, not a winnerThe two metal columns are within one per cent of each other, and both numbers behind that margin are marked in the model as not re-verified: the rent per machine, and how many hours renting actually saves. Treat them as the same answer with different cash flows, and let your own quote break the tie.
The residualAfter sixty months the owned machines are five years old and still working — hardware of this class is routinely run for seven or eight. What you hold is a fleet with years left in it, counted here at a conservative fifteen per cent of what it cost. It is also the whole of the difference between owning and renting at this size: strip the residual out and the two columns are the same number. You do not own hardware to save money against renting it. You own it when the fleet is big enough to carry the room, and to stop asking somebody else for permission.

Before you sign anything

Almost nobody should sign a facility contract before they have run this stack once. 3 refurbished machines on a managed switch under a desk cost about $3,430 once and $30 a month in electricity, and they run the whole of Stage 3 unchanged. It saves nothing, and that is not what it is for: it is the smallest bet that tells you whether the rest of this book is for you.

Every figure on this page is a dated list-price observation, not a quotation. Cloud prices are on-demand and undiscounted on purpose; a reader with a commitment discount should substitute their own effective rate, and a reader without one is genuinely paying this.

The rented column, with a warning The monthly rent of one dedicated machine is the single figure in this book that a re-check could not settle: the cheapest European provider and a survey of three others disagree by a factor of two or three on the same specification, which is wide enough to decide the question on its own. The number above is the last one observed and it is not a quotation. Get a quote before you let this column decide anything — Move 07 step 6 is where to do it.
